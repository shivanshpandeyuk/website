*A Cambridge Student Investment Fund pitch from December 2024. Portfolio manager: Shivansh Pandey; analysts Josh Tan, Arabella Ann-Kyng and Jasmine Wong. The figures and investment view below are from that pitch; check them against current filings.*

**Tokyo Electron (TSE:8035) — Long. Target price \$270.58, 82% upside and a 16.8% IRR in the bullish case; the base case is \$239.80, 61.2% upside and a 14.1% IRR [E, original December 2024 pitch].**

## Industry overview

Tokyo Electron has a **7.9% market share in US semiconductor machine manufacturing and 12% globally [U, as stated in the pitch]**. The pitch is built on semiconductors: chip demand is set to rise over the coming decade, and the global semiconductor manufacturing equipment market is set to grow at a **CAGR of 10.4% from \$91.20bn in 2023 to \$182.30bn [U, as stated in the pitch]**.

TEL is the **fourth-largest company in Japan by market capitalisation [U, as stated in the pitch]** and one of the big five equipment manufacturers. The combined market cap of the five most valuable Japanese semiconductor manufacturers has doubled in dollar terms over the past year. The key trends are advanced manufacturing technologies (lithography, etching and deposition; EUV systems with higher numerical apertures for smaller feature sizes), advanced packaging (System-in-Package, Fan-Out Wafer-Level Packaging, 3D stacking) and continued miniaturisation and node advancement towards 7nm and 5nm.

## Outperforms in every cycle

For a long-term investor, semiconductors are a natural choice. Semiconductor, semiconductor capital equipment and EDA stocks have outperformed across **3, 5, 10 and 15-year horizons [U, as stated in the pitch]**; semiconductors remain integral to the technology value chain, driving sustained demand; and structural profitability and free-cash-flow generation have improved.

## Company overview

TEL is the only company that produces the equipment for all four key wafer front-end processes:

- **Deposition** — metal thin films deposited on the wafer surface before device structures and wiring are formed; various deposition systems (oxide/anneal, CVD, ASFD, ALD, PVD).
- **Lithography** — transferring fine patterns onto wafer surfaces through photoresist coating, exposure and development. TEL holds a **90% share of the overall coater/developer market and roughly 100% share of the high-NA process [R, as stated in the pitch]**.
- **Etching** — defining the circuit pattern formed by lithography; TEL has the second-largest market share in dry etch.
- **Cleaning** — removing foreign objects to prevent process defects; TEL has the second-largest market share of wafer cleaning systems.

TEL also makes flat-panel-display manufacturing equipment. At the Q3 capitalisation used in the pitch: share price **\$147**, **461mn** shares outstanding, market cap **\$67,731mn**, less cash and equivalents of **\$3,668mn** and **total debt of \$0**, for an enterprise value of **\$64,062mn [R, as stated in the pitch]**. The projected income statement carries a **50% dividend payout in every year to 2027E [E, original pitch]**.

## Thesis

The pitch rests on three points: Tokyo Electron's competitive advantage cannot be eroded; the market is discounting semiconductors but does not recognise the glut is over; and Japanese strength is not being recognised.

### 1. Competitive advantage cannot be eroded

TEL has a monopoly in the four-stage patterning process. Almost every fab in the world has TEL machines, and the firm's R&D/capex feedback loop is at the heart of its advantage. Each of the big five has the same business model, with niches in different steps of the process — ASML, for example, holds a monopoly specifically in EUV lithography.

In the 1980s, TEL underinvested because of poor economic conditions; Korean manufacturers did the opposite and were able to penetrate the equipment market. Management does not intend to repeat that mistake: R&D and capex are both set to double over the previous five years, with R&D spend rising from **¥220bn in FY2025 to ¥600bn in FY2029 — roughly doubling R&D to \$10bn over the next five years [E, original pitch]**. Product innovation comes through iterative reductions in product power usage and R&D aimed at miniaturisation (higher-layer NAND memory and finer nodes); process innovation comes through **roughly 100% employee retention [R, as stated in the pitch]**, which accumulates knowledge, and a supply chain refined over decades.

Historically TEL traded at roughly the same P/E as its peers; at the time of the pitch it traded at about a **six-turn discount to that peer set [R, as stated in the pitch]**. The pitch argues the June AI rally over-emphasised TEL's AI exposure and the subsequent price correction was too steep. The sell-side agreed with the income projections but disagreed on price.

### 2. The glut is over

Semiconductors are a cyclical industry: a shortage through 2021–22, a glut through 2022–23, and positive growth resuming in 2024. Semiconductor capital equipment demand follows semiconductor volumes at roughly a **six-month lag [U, as stated in the pitch]**, which implies the upturn in semicap equipment is now. The pitch places the cycle **30–35% into the typical two-year upturn phase [E, original pitch]**. The upcycle is signalled by **ten months of year-on-year growth [U, as stated in the pitch]**, with acceleration expected in 2025 from edge-AI adoption, non-AI chip restocking after the glut, and favourable macro conditions. Historical cycles last **26–30 months [U, as stated in the pitch]**, and the sector had pulled back to **18% below its all-time-high P/E [U, as stated in the pitch]**.

### 3. Japanese strength is not being recognised

The pitch argues the Japanese economy is at an inflection point: inflation supports margin expansion for firms with pricing power, higher household spending as nominal wages rise, and lower real rates after the Bank of Japan held rates and the yen slid. Japan also has specific endowments — universities at the forefront of wafer-fab research (OIST's cheaper EUV lithography method), and the largest pool of skilled, lower-cost technicians and engineers. On policy, Japan's semiconductor subsidy-to-GDP ratio is the highest in the world at **0.7% [U, as stated in the pitch]**, corporate reforms have simplified acquisition rules and expanded NISA, and TEL is exempt from US sanctions while still benefiting from the CHIPS Act. Japan has been relatively undervalued on a P/E basis for over a decade.

## Valuation

The DCF uses a **10% discount rate, a 15.0x EV/EBITDA exit multiple, a 10% financing rate and a 6% reinvestment rate [E, original pitch]**. Against a reference price of **\$148.80 [U, original pitch]**, the implied share price is **\$270.58 in the bullish case (82% upside, 16.8% IRR), \$239.80 in the base case (61.2% upside, 14.1% IRR), \$191.30 in the bearish case (28.6% upside, 9.3% IRR) and \$135.52 in the worst case (−8.9%, 2.7% IRR) [E, original pitch]**.

## Catalyst path

Near-term catalysts include record capex (**TSMC's \$32bn for 2024 [E, as stated in the pitch]**), the US CHIPS Act (**\$52bn in subsidies [U, as stated in the pitch]**) and EUV momentum (**ASML projecting EUV shipments up 30% in 2024 [E, as stated in the pitch]**). Through FY2025–26: Japan's domestic investment as Rapidus begins a **\$37bn fab [E, as stated in the pitch]**, China's mature-node expansion as its fab spending exceeds **\$17bn [E, as stated in the pitch]**, and a sustainability push as TEL commits to **10% more energy-efficient equipment [E, as stated in the pitch]** for TSMC and Samsung. Longer term: market-share expansion, with TEL targeting a **25% global share of the \$10bn wafer-cleaning-tools market [E, as stated in the pitch]**, and global semiconductor revenue projected to **surpass \$1 trillion by 2030, doubling TEL's addressable market [U, as stated in the pitch]**.

## Risks and mitigants

The pitch identifies three risks. A delayed rebound in end-product demand, given revenue is tied to the semiconductor cycle, which can cut either way; the mitigant is TEL's focus on high-growth segments such as automotive and AI/IoT chips. Geopolitical and export-control risk from US–China tensions, though TEL is less exposed to China than its peers; the mitigant is expanding the customer base in Taiwan and South Korea, which house TSMC and Samsung. And rapid technological change and competition from ASML, Applied Materials and Lam Research; the mitigant is aggressive R&D, including large investments in EUV-compatible processes.
