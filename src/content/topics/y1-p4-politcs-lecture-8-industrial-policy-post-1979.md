**Margaret Thatcher** (1979)
Principal Rationale -  **decentralised system of resource allocation achieves productive and allocative efficiency (Pareto optimality),** thus the privatised **industries should become more efficient if subject to market forces** and **no longer in receipt of substantial government subsidy**.

Aiming to encourage a market driven sectoral-shift from an economy based on **manufacturing and the extractive industries** to a **retail and financial service sector based economy.** <--- much more technically efficient, had far greater growth potential than the sunset industries in structural decline (Transition slowed down by nationalisation and regional policy).

Whereas most post-war governments propped them up with subsidies etc. Thatcher's removal of such support *accelerated* the structural decline of these industries.

**Building a 'share holding democracy'**
1. Good for electorate - instils a culture of a shareholding democracy, making more favourable attitudes to the capitalist system. By having a pecuniary interest (dividends) in ensuring shareholder value is maximised. (i.e. help develop a broader portfolio of stocks and shares - never materialised in the end)
2. When labour government returns to government, because the utilities had been nationalised and owned by the public they would not be able to reverse the change.
- Shares to private investors were sold at less than their anticipated market value in order to encourage sales, and shares were sold in small quantities to prevent the institutional share holders from purchasing them.
- dividends generated for shareholders would create a more general culture of share ownership,

**Attracting FDI**
This was the policy of attracting mainly American and Japanese businesses to locate in Britain to create fresh employment. It was also a policy of ensuring that Britain was increasingly integrated into the global capitalist system, and reliant upon businesses from other capitalist countries. The purpose of this was to ensure that no future Labour government could reverse the policy

**Deliberate policy of reducing employment in coal/sunset industries**
- By moving back on the left axis, the MRP of labour rises - this should mean that these smaller businesses finally achieve profitability (at least on marginal terms) and so can be sold over to private hands.
- *Termination of companies under private hands less liable for government*

**Injecting Competition into Natural monopolies**
Because the utilities are natural monopolies, it was technically difficult to introduce competition.
- The electricity generating system was broken up into a duopoly of coal fired producers called National Power and PowerGen,
- The distribution system was still a natural monopoly, and although it was broken up into Regional Electricity Companies (RECs) 
- Yet the monopoly issue remained
- Hence the creation of quangos (ofcom, oftel etc.) that regulated the privatised utilities to prevent them exploiting their monopoly power by substantially increasing prices to customers.

**Weakening trade unions**
- Nicholas Ridley, one of Thatcher’s cabinet Ministers who produced a report for breaking up the public sector and weakening the trades unions

**Privatisation**
*The principal organisations which the Thatcher government privatised were: Associated British Ports (1983, 1984), British Aerospace (1981, 1985), British Airports Authority (1987), British Airways (1987), British Gas (1986), British Leyland (1981-1988), British Shipbuilders (1985-1989), British Steel (1988), British Telecom (1984, 1991, 1993), Cable and Wireless (1981, 1983, 1985), Municipal Bus Companies (1988) National Bus Company (1986-1988), Rolls-Royce (1987), Water Companies (1989).*

**North Sea Oil**
- Reserves of oil and gas in the North Sea had been known for some time. Serious exploration began in the 1960s and in 1969 the US company Amoco discovered the Montrose oilfield, followed by the discovery of the giant Forties field in 1970.
- Volatile weather conditions made drilling particularly hazardous and very expensive; by the 1980s costs for developing the necessary new technologies far exceeded NASA’s budget to land a man on the moon.
- It was only the **OPEC oil price rises of 1973-4 and 1977 which made North Sea oil production viable** 
- Labour government wanted state control of the sale of north sea oil, but development of the industry occurred in Thatcher's years.
- Instead, tax yield from oil production provided a significant source of revenue for the Thatcher government as well as for subsequent governments. 
- **The Thatcher government used North Sea oil tax receipts to finance government expenditure as unemployment grew and as she was averse to deficit financing.** This was criticised by Labour who wanted the revenues spent on infrastructure projects (to promote long term growth).
- The oil and gas sector is currently Britain’s largest corporation tax payer contributing 16.4% of total Government corporation tax receipts.

**John Major** (1990)
- Rail industry reform - This government decided to break up the rail industry into a private company which would own and operate the track and signalling equipment named ‘Railtrack’, and offer regional franchises at competitive tender for train operating companies to provide the train services.
- Privatisations - John Major’s government would privatise the Central Electricity Generating Board (1990- 95), British Coal (1994), and British Rail (1995-97).
- Heseltine (tarzan) attempted to privatise the Post office (failed unpopular). *Would be done subsequently by David Cameron*

**Tony Blair** (1997)

**Continuity of Privatisation Programme**
- To persuade labour as 'electable' shortly after their election they extended privatisation to the air traffic control system (purely symbolic, not going to have any perceptible effect on improving the efficiency of macroeconomy as a whole - it is a **signal that nationalisation will not return**). 
- *The influential Labour politician, Peter Mandelson declared, “we are all Thatcherites now”.*
- A series of train accidents prompted the Blair government to replace Major's ‘Railtrack’ <--- disjointed structure made maintenance an issue.
	- Replaced with a new organisation: ‘Network Rail’, neither a private business nor a nationalised company, but a not-for-profit organisation, to bring maintenance in-house and reduce such accidents.

**Regulation**
Although New Labour relinquished the notion of the ownership of assets in order to control the allocation of resources, it replaced this notion by that of regulation. The degree to which the Labour government re-regulated the private sector was quite substantial.

E.g. Re-introducing employer protection (see labour redundancy programme) - In the case of a redundancy, s/he would be invited to interview by the employer and could bring her/his legal representative or trades union representative with them. Only if the employer was able to show that under the law there was a case for redundancy, could the proposal proceed. This policy was intended to increase the costs to employers and thus deter redundancies.

**Public-Private Partnerships** <-- to bring private finance to public provision
*to keep the neoliberal revolution going even after most industries privatised*
- A PPP is where capital projects are financed by the private sector and the services provided are purchased through a contract (usually 30 years) by government
- Although this reduces the public liability for investment funds *ab initio*, the policy has been criticised on the grounds that the private sector successfully **shifts risk to the public sector** and **wealth to the private sector** - so it is more expensive for the tax payer in the long-run.
- *to increase private sector involvement in public service provision* In practice, the Blair and Brown Labour governments were keen on it for a different reason: to enable them to provide more public service provision while shifting the cost to future generations

**Gordon Brown + Financial Crisis**
- During the sub-prime mortgage crisis of 2008, **Gordon Brown’s Labour government was forced to nationalise failing British banks, not out of a doctrinal shift back to socialism (despite his instincts as a socialist), but because of their imminent collapse**.
